# Transformer Encoder–Decoder: A Simple Story 🌍

## 1. The Big Picture 

Most neural sequence models follow a simple idea:

> **Input → Understanding → Output**

In technical terms:

> **x₁...xₙ → z₁...zₙ → y₁...yₘ**

- **x₁...xₙ** → Input words  
- **z₁...zₙ** → Internal understanding (encoded meaning)  
- **y₁...yₘ** → Output words  

👉 Note: Input and output lengths can be different!

---

## 2. Think of It Like a Translator 

Imagine a translator at the United Nations:

---

### 🔷 Encoder = The Listener 

- Reads the **entire input sentence**
- Understands its meaning deeply  
- Converts it into a **numerical representation**

👉 This understanding is:

> **z = (z₁, z₂, ..., zₙ)**

Think of this as:
> A compressed “understanding” of the sentence

---

### 🔷 Decoder = The Speaker 

- Takes the encoder’s understanding  
- Starts generating the output **one word at a time**

---

## 3. The Auto-Regressive Magic 🔁

This is the key idea:

> The decoder uses its **own previous outputs** to generate the next word

---

### Example:

Translate:

> "I am happy" → "मैं खुश हूँ"

| Step | Decoder Input                      | Output |
|------|----------------------------------|--------|
| 1    | [START] + encoder understanding  | मैं    |
| 2    | मैं + encoder understanding      | खुश   |
| 3    | मैं खुश + encoder understanding | हूँ    |
| 4    | मैं खुश हूँ + encoder           | [END]  |

👉 Each word feeds into the next step  

This is called:

> **Auto-Regressive Generation**

---

## 4. Important Clarification 

- The output is **NOT fed back into the encoder**
- It is fed **back into the decoder itself**

👉 That’s what makes it **auto-regressive**

---

## 5. What Makes Transformers Special? 

Transformers follow this same encoder-decoder idea, but with a twist:

> They use **Self-Attention everywhere**

---

### 🔷 Two Main Components:

#### 1. Stacked Self-Attention 

- Multiple attention layers stacked on top of each other  
- Each layer refines understanding  

👉 Like reading a sentence:
- First pass → basic idea  
- Second pass → deeper meaning  
- Third pass → full clarity  

---

#### 2. Pointwise Fully Connected Layers 

- After attention connects words  
- Each word is processed **individually**

👉 Think of it as:
- Attention → "These words are related"  
- FC Layer → "Let me think deeper about each word"

---

## 6. Final Intuition 

> Encoder understands → compresses into meaning (z)  
> Decoder speaks → one word at a time using past outputs + understanding  

---

## 7. One-Line Summary 💡

> The encoder reads and understands the sentence, and the decoder generates the output step-by-step using both the encoder’s knowledge and its own past words.

# The Transformer Architecture — Full Flow

## Example

We will follow one example throughout:

> Input: "I am happy"  
> Output: "मैं खुश हूँ"

---

# 1. Encoder (Input Processing)

## Step 1 — Input Embedding

Each word is converted into a fixed-size vector:

- "I" → 512-dimensional vector  
- "am" → 512-dimensional vector  
- "happy" → 512-dimensional vector  

These vectors capture semantic meaning in numerical form.

---

## Step 2 — Positional Encoding

Since the model processes all words in parallel, it has no inherent sense of order.

Positional encoding adds position information to each word:

- "I" → embedding + position 1  
- "am" → embedding + position 2  
- "happy" → embedding + position 3  

This ensures the model understands sequence structure.

---

## Step 3 — Multi-Head Self-Attention

Each word attends to every other word:

- "I" attends to "am" and "happy"  
- "am" attends to "I" and "happy"  
- "happy" attends to "I" and "am"  

This builds contextual understanding.

### Multi-Head Attention

Attention is computed multiple times in parallel (e.g., 8 heads), each focusing on different aspects such as:

- Grammatical relationships  
- Semantic meaning  
- Positional patterns  

---

## Step 4 — Add & Normalize

Each sublayer output is combined with its input:

\[
\text{Output} = \text{LayerNorm}(x + \text{Sublayer}(x))
\]

- Residual connection preserves original information  
- Normalization stabilizes training  

---

## Step 5 — Feed Forward Network

Each position is processed independently through a neural network.

- Attention captures relationships  
- Feed-forward refines individual representations  

---

## Step 6 — Add & Normalize

Same operation applied after the feed-forward layer.

---

## Step 7 — Repeat Encoder Blocks

Steps 3–6 are repeated **N = 6 times**.

Each layer progressively refines the representation:

- Early layers → basic relationships  
- Middle layers → structure and context  
- Final layers → deep semantic understanding  

---

## Encoder Output

The encoder produces:

\[
z = (z_1, z_2, z_3)
\]

Each vector is 512-dimensional and represents the input sentence in a rich, contextual form.

---

# 2. Decoder (Output Generation)

## Step 8 — Output Embedding

The decoder starts with a special token:

- [START] → embedding vector  

As words are generated, they are also embedded.

---

## Step 9 — Positional Encoding

Position information is added to decoder inputs, same as in the encoder.

---

## Step 10 — Masked Multi-Head Self-Attention

The decoder uses masked attention to prevent access to future tokens.

### Masking Behavior

| Position | Can Attend To            | Cannot Attend To |
|----------|--------------------------|------------------|
| 1        | [START]                  | Future tokens    |
| 2        | [START], word₁           | Future tokens    |
| 3        | [START], word₁, word₂    | Future tokens    |

This enforces:

> Each position can only depend on previous positions and itself.

---

## Step 11 — Add & Normalize

Residual connection and normalization applied.

---

## Step 12 — Cross Attention (Encoder–Decoder Attention)

The decoder attends to the encoder output.

- Queries come from the decoder  
- Keys and values come from the encoder output  

This allows the decoder to focus on relevant parts of the input while generating each word.

---

## Step 13 — Add & Normalize

Same residual and normalization step.

---

## Step 14 — Feed Forward Network

Independent transformation applied to each position.

---

## Step 15 — Add & Normalize

Final normalization within the decoder block.

---

## Step 16 — Repeat Decoder Blocks

Steps 10–15 are repeated **N = 6 times**.

Each layer refines the generated representation further.

---

# 3. Output Generation

## Step 17 — Linear Layer

Transforms the 512-dimensional vector into a vocabulary-sized vector.

Example:

\[
512 \rightarrow 50{,}000
\]

Each value represents the score for a word in the vocabulary.

---

## Step 18 — Softmax

Converts scores into probabilities:

- Highest probability word is selected as output  

Example:

- "मैं" → highest probability → selected  

---

# 4. Full Flow Summary

```

"I am happy"
↓
[Embedding + Positional Encoding]
↓
[6 × Encoder Blocks]
↓
Encoder Output (z)
↓
[6 × Decoder Blocks]
↓
[Linear Layer]
↓
[Softmax]
↓
"मैं" → "खुश" → "हूँ" → [END]

```

---

# 5. Key Takeaways

- Encoder processes the entire input in parallel  
- Decoder generates output step-by-step (auto-regressive)  
- Masking prevents access to future tokens  
- Cross-attention connects input and output  
- Residual connections preserve information  
- Layer normalization ensures stability  
- All representations maintain a fixed dimension (512)

---

# 6. One-Line Summary

The Transformer encodes the input into a rich representation and decodes it step-by-step using attention mechanisms, enabling fully parallel processing and efficient sequence modeling.

# 4. Scaling the Embeddings

The paper multiplies every embedding vector by

$$\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}}$$

For the original Transformer:

$$d_{\text{model}} = 512$$

Therefore:

$$\sqrt{512} \approx 22.62$$


Every embedding vector is multiplied by approximately **22.6** before positional encoding is added.

---

## Why is this Scaling Necessary?

Immediately after the embedding layer, the Transformer adds **Positional Encoding**.

```
Final Input

=
Embedding
+
Positional Encoding
```

Positional encoding values are relatively small (typically between **−1 and 1** because they are generated using sine and cosine functions).

During training, learned embedding values can also become quite small.

Without scaling, positional information could dominate the word meaning.

Example:

Without scaling:

```
Embedding           = 0.02
Position Encoding   = 0.80

Final = 0.82
```

Most of the information now comes from the position rather than the word itself.

After scaling:

```
Embedding

0.02 × 22.6

≈ 0.45

Position Encoding

0.80

Final

1.25
```

Now both **word meaning** and **position information** contribute meaningfully.

Scaling ensures that neither dominates the other.

---

# 5. From Decoder Output to the Next Word

After passing through all decoder layers, the Transformer has a refined representation of the current position.

However, this representation is still only a **512-dimensional vector**.

Example:

```
Decoder Output

↓

[0.12, -0.43, 0.91, ..., 512 values]
```

Humans need a **word**, not a vector.

The Transformer therefore performs two additional steps.

---

## Step 1 — Linear Transformation

The decoder output is multiplied by a learned weight matrix.

```
512 dimensions

↓

Vocabulary Size
```

If the vocabulary contains 50,000 words,

```
512

↓

50,000 scores
```

Each score corresponds to one possible word.

Example:

| Word | Score |
|------|------:|
| मैं | 8.72 |
| वह | 2.31 |
| खुश | 1.45 |
| हूँ | 0.92 |

These values are called **logits** (raw prediction scores).

They are **not probabilities**.

They can be negative, positive, or any real number.

---

## Step 2 — Softmax

Softmax converts these raw scores into probabilities.

Example:

Before Softmax

```
मैं   = 8.72
वह    = 2.31
खुश   = 1.45
हूँ   = 0.92
```

After Softmax

| Word | Probability |
|------|------------:|
| मैं | 0.82 |
| वह | 0.09 |
| खुश | 0.05 |
| हूँ | 0.04 |

The probabilities always sum to **1**.

The model simply selects the word with the highest probability.

```
Highest Probability

↓

Next Generated Token
```

The entire decoding process then repeats for the next word.

---

# 6. Weight Sharing

One elegant optimization introduced in the Transformer is **weight sharing**.

Instead of learning three completely different matrices, the model reuses the same learned matrix for:

- Input Embedding
- Output Embedding
- Pre-Softmax Linear Transformation

---

## Why Does This Work?

The embedding matrix has shape

```
Vocabulary Size × 512
```

The pre-softmax linear layer requires

```
512 × Vocabulary Size
```

Notice that one is simply the **transpose** of the other.

This means the same learned relationship between

```
Word

↔

Vector
```

can be used in both directions.

---

## Intuition

Think of a bilingual dictionary.

One side maps

```
English

↓

Hindi
```

The other side maps

```
Hindi

↓

English
```

The knowledge is the same—the direction changes.

Similarly,

The embedding layer converts

```
Word

↓

Vector
```

while the pre-softmax layer converts

```
Vector

↓

Word
```

using the same learned representation.

---

## Benefits of Weight Sharing

Weight sharing provides several advantages.

- Reduces the total number of parameters.
- Saves memory.
- Improves consistency between input and output representations.
- Acts as a form of regularization, reducing overfitting.

# Key Concepts

| Component | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| Embedding Layer | Converts token IDs into meaningful vectors |
| d_model | Size of every token representation (512 in the original Transformer) |
| Scaling by √d_model | Balances embedding magnitude with positional encoding |
| Linear Layer | Converts decoder output into vocabulary scores (logits) |
| Softmax | Converts logits into probabilities |
| Weight Sharing | Uses the same learned matrix for embeddings and the output projection |

---

# Summary

Embeddings transform discrete token IDs into rich **512-dimensional semantic vectors** that the Transformer can understand. Before positional information is added, these embeddings are scaled by **√d_model** to maintain a healthy balance between word meaning and positional encoding.

After the decoder produces its final 512-dimensional representation, a shared linear transformation projects it into the vocabulary space, generating one score for every possible word. The **softmax function** then converts these scores into probabilities, allowing the model to predict the most likely next token. By sharing the same weight matrix between the embedding layers and the output projection, the Transformer reduces parameters, improves consistency, and makes learning more efficient.




# Layer-by-Layer Comparison

## 1. Self-Attention

### Complexity

$
O(n^2 \cdot d)
$

Every token attends to every other token.

For a sequence of **n** words, there are:

$
n \times n
$

attention scores to compute.

Each score involves vectors of dimension **d**.

### Sequential Operations

$
O(1)
$

All attention scores are computed simultaneously.

The entire sequence can be processed in parallel.

### Maximum Path Length

$
O(1)
$

Every token has a direct connection to every other token through the attention mechanism.

Example:

```
Word 1  <────────────► Word 100
```

Only one attention step separates them.

---

### Advantages

- Fully parallelizable
- Captures long-range dependencies easily
- Shortest possible information path

### Disadvantage

Quadratic computation makes very long sequences expensive.

Example:

```
10 words
10 × 10 = 100 attention scores

1000 words
1000 × 1000 = 1,000,000 attention scores
```

---

## 2. Recurrent Networks (RNNs)

### Complexity

$
O(n \cdot d^2)
$

Each token is processed sequentially using matrix multiplications involving the hidden state.

---

### Sequential Operations

$
O(n)
$

Tokens cannot be processed simultaneously.

Processing order is:

```
Word1
   ↓
Word2
   ↓
Word3
   ↓
...
Word n
```

Every token depends on the previous one.

---

### Maximum Path Length

$
O(n)
$

Information must travel through every intermediate hidden state.

Example:

```
Word1
 ↓
Word2
 ↓
Word3
 ↓
...
 ↓
Word100
```

Long-distance information gradually weakens, making it difficult to model long-range dependencies.

---

### Advantages

- Naturally handles sequential data

### Disadvantages

- Cannot be parallelized
- Long information paths
- Suffers from vanishing gradients over long sequences

---

## 3. Convolutional Networks (CNNs)

### Complexity

$
O(k \cdot n \cdot d^2)
$

where

- **k** = kernel size

A convolution processes only a local neighborhood around each token.

---

### Sequential Operations

$
O(1)
$

Different positions can be processed simultaneously.

---

### Maximum Path Length

$
O(\log_k(n))
$

A single convolution only sees nearby tokens.

To connect distant words, multiple convolution layers must be stacked.

Example:

```
Layer 1
Word1 ── Word2 ── Word3

Layer 2
Word1 ───────── Word3

Layer 3
Word1 ───────────────── Word5
```

Information gradually spreads across layers.

---

### Advantages

- Parallelizable
- Efficient for local patterns

### Disadvantages

- Requires multiple layers to capture long-range relationships

---

## 4. Restricted Self-Attention

Instead of attending to every token, each token attends only to nearby neighbors.

Suppose the attention window size is **r**.

Each token only communicates with **r** surrounding tokens.

---

### Complexity

$
O(r \cdot n \cdot d)
$

This is much cheaper than full self-attention because

$
r \ll n
$

---

### Sequential Operations

$
O(1)
$

The computation remains fully parallel.

---

### Maximum Path Length

$
O\left(\frac{n}{r}\right)
$

Information travels gradually through overlapping attention windows.

Example:

```
Window 1:
1 2 3

Window 2:
3 4 5

Window 3:
5 6 7
```

A token cannot directly reach distant tokens.

Instead, information propagates through neighboring windows.

---

### Advantages

- Much lower computational cost
- Parallelizable

### Disadvantages

- Longer information paths
- Cannot model global relationships as effectively as full self-attention

---

# Comparison Table

| Layer Type | Complexity per Layer | Sequential Operations | Maximum Path Length |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Self-Attention** | $O(n^2 \cdot d)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(1)$ |
| **Recurrent** | $O(n \cdot d^2)$ | $O(n)$ | $O(n)$ |
| **Convolutional** | $O(k \cdot n \cdot d^2)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(\log_k(n))$ |
| **Restricted Self-Attention** | $O(r \cdot n \cdot d)$ | $O(1)$ | $O(n/r)$ |
---

# Why Did the Transformer Choose Self-Attention?

Although self-attention has quadratic computational complexity, it provides two significant advantages:

1. **Complete parallelism**

   Every token is processed simultaneously, making training highly efficient on modern hardware.

2. **Shortest information path**

   Any token can directly attend to any other token in a single step.

These properties make it much easier to learn long-range dependencies than recurrent or convolutional architectures.

For typical NLP tasks, where sequence lengths are moderate, these benefits outweigh the increased computational cost.

---

# Summary

Self-attention trades higher computational complexity for significantly better parallelism and the shortest possible communication path between tokens. Compared with recurrent and convolutional architectures, it enables more efficient training and better modeling of long-range dependencies, making it the foundation of the Transformer architecture.
