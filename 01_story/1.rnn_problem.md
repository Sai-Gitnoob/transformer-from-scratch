# Chapter 1: The RNN Problem

*Before we meet the hero of our story — Attention — we need to understand the villain it defeated: the humble RNN.*

---

## What Is an RNN?

**RNN = Recurrent Neural Network.**

It's a type of neural network built to handle **sequential data** — things that come in order, like words in a sentence — by remembering what it has already seen.

### A Simple Analogy

Imagine reading this sentence, one word at a time:

> "The cat sat on the mat because it was tired."

As you read:

- **"The"** → you note it
- **"cat"** → you now remember *"The cat"*
- **"sat"** → you remember *"The cat sat"*
- ...and so on

By the time you reach the word **"it,"** you already remember **"cat"** from earlier — so you instantly know *"it"* refers to the cat.

That's exactly how an RNN works. It reads input **one step at a time**, and carries forward a kind of memory — called a **hidden state** — from each step to the next.

> **In one line:** An RNN processes sequences by passing memory from one step to the next.

---

## How That Memory Actually Works

At every step `t`, the RNN computes a hidden state `hₜ`. But here's the catch — to compute `hₜ`, it *needs* `hₜ₋₁`, the hidden state from the step right before it.

Think of it like a **relay race**: you can't run your leg of the race until you receive the baton from the runner before you. That baton is the hidden state — and every runner (every step) is stuck waiting for it.

This chain works fine for short sentences. But it creates two serious problems as things scale up.

---

## Problem 1: RNNs Forget Over Long Distances

Basic ("vanilla") RNNs are bad at holding onto information across long gaps.

> "The cat, which was very tired after playing all day, sat on the mat because it was sleepy."

By the time a basic RNN reaches **"it,"** it may have already forgotten about **"cat"** — too much has happened in between.

**The fix at the time:** smarter versions of the RNN were built —

- **LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory):** uses internal "gates" that decide what to remember and what to forget, so important information can survive long gaps.
- **GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit):** a lighter, faster version of LSTM that keeps most of the same memory benefits.

These became the **state-of-the-art** tools for tasks like:

| Task | Example |
|---|---|
| **Sequence modeling** | Understanding "I am going to school" — word order matters |
| **Transduction** | Converting one sequence to another, e.g. "Hello" → "Bonjour" |
| **Language modeling** | Predicting the next word: "The cat sat on the ___" → *mat* |
| **Machine translation** | "I love AI" → "J'aime l'IA" |

Most of these systems used an **encoder-decoder** structure:

- **Encoder** — reads the input sentence and compresses it into a summary
- **Decoder** — takes that summary and generates the output sentence

Think of the encoder as *understanding*, and the decoder as *speaking*.

---

## Problem 2: The Chain Kills Speed

Even with LSTM and GRU fixing the memory issue, a deeper structural problem remained — one that had nothing to do with memory and everything to do with *order*.

Because every hidden state `hₜ` depends on `hₜ₋₁`, the model is forced into a strict chain:

> Step 3 waits for Step 2. Step 2 waits for Step 1. You can never jump ahead.

This is called **sequential dependency**, and it causes real trouble:

- **No parallelization.** Modern hardware — GPUs — is built to do thousands of calculations *at the same time*. But an RNN can't take advantage of that, because each step needs the previous one finished first. It's forced to process everything one at a time, which makes training slow.
- **Longer sequences make it worse.** More words mean more sequential steps, more memory required, and a harder time learning relationships between things that are far apart in the sentence.

---

## The Patches That Helped — But Didn't Solve It

Researchers tried to ease these problems without abandoning the RNN structure:

- **Factorization tricks:** Break large weight matrices into smaller ones. Same math, less memory, faster computation — like simplifying one big calculation into a few smaller, manageable ones.
- **Conditional computation:** Instead of activating the *entire* network for every input, only the relevant parts turn on. It's like a hospital that only calls in the specialist you actually need, rather than every doctor on staff.

Both approaches genuinely helped — models got faster and lighter, and conditional computation even improved performance in some cases.

**But neither one touched the real bottleneck.**

The sequential chain — one step waiting on the one before it — was still there. No matter how you optimized around it, you were still processing your sentence **one word at a time.**

---

## The Turning Point

At Google, a group of researchers asked a question that changed everything:

> *"What if we remove recurrence completely — and rely only on attention?"*

That single idea — that a model might not need to read step-by-step at all — became the seed of the **Transformer**.

> Sometimes the biggest breakthroughs don't come from improving the old idea.
> They come from asking: *what if we removed it entirely?*
